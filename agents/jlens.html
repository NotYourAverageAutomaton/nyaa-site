J-Lens

Status: paused. Updated 2026-11-21.

Can you read the hidden steps of a model's thinking from its middle layers?

Where we stand

Paused while we harden the agents. The latest test passed on 21 November; the next is written.

Paused while we harden the agents. The latest test passed on 21 November 2026, against a bar written before the run. The next test, J54 (whether the hidden step drives the answer), is written and will be frozen before it runs, when that work is done.

What worked

The lens reads the hidden step. On prompts whose answer needs an unspoken step ("the color of the planet fourth from the Sun is" needs "Mars"), our lens surfaced the hidden step more often than the plain logit lens (a gain of 0.10, 95% CI 0.08 to 0.13), and far more often than a decoy from another prompt in the same task set (a gain of 0.29, 95% CI 0.27 to 0.32). The shuffled lens, which had to fail, did.

Not on every set. On typo prompts our lens did worse than the plain logit lens (-0.089, 95% CI -0.158 to -0.019), and on poetry it did not beat its decoy (+0.025, 95% CI -0.024 to 0.074). The gain over the logit lens also cleared zero on random states (+0.034), so the gain over the decoy is what carries the result.

The differences: our map is fitted on 54 documents (the paper: about 1,000); it reads toward the final layer (the paper: the penultimate); positions under 16 were skipped in the fit; 7 strided layers; no tuned-lens baseline; and nothing causal yet.

A small dose helps, on a stricter measure. A blend that's mostly the logit lens with a small dose of the map lands about 8% closer to the model's own final answer on fresh text. We're only reporting that one; we didn't set a pass mark for it in advance.

The result: an average gain of -0.041 (95% CI -0.077 to -0.007). It fails all three. We're sure it's not better. We can't say it's worse: grouped by passage, the interval spans zero.

We had predicted a gain of 0.25 to 1.30. That was wrong, and it came from stretching a curve well past the range we'd measured.

Results so far, run by run

At mid-depth, neither the Jacobian lens nor the logit lens predicts upcoming tokens better than word frequency does. Only the trained lens beats frequency, and only for the next token.

What it is

We build the lens from a set of documents. It's a map from each middle layer of the model to the model's final answer.

Where it came from

A 2026 paper, 'Verbalizable Representations Form a Global Workspace in Language Models' (Gurnee et al., arXiv 2607.15495), introduced the Jacobian lens, a way to read a model's middle layers as words.

Our idea

A finding like that is only as strong as the attempts to repeat it on other models. So we tried it on a different one, Swift 1.5 27B, a fine-tune of Qwen 3.8 27B. We wrote the pass marks down before each run, so a success and a failure would both count.

We won't run a Stage 3 on this design. Instead we asked the paper's own question of the lens we already had, and that test passed (below). Next we test whether the hidden steps it reads actually drive the answer. That test is written down but has not run.

How we test it

Since 21 November our test is the hidden-step readout under Where we stand. The diagram below shows our first test, next-token prediction from the middle layers, which we no longer use to judge the lens.

Then we score it on six documents it has never seen. We check how often its top guess is among the text's next tokens (a token is roughly a word), compared with a plain guess based on how common each word is and with the logit lens. We also check how far it lands from the model's final answer.

Every pass mark is written down before the run, and a different agent, one that didn't run the test, grades it. When a number turns out to be wrong, the correction stays in the repo next to the claim.

Up to Stage 2 we scored the lens on how well it agreed with the model's next word. That was the wrong measure (see What we got wrong). The next test scores hidden steps instead.

We set the bar before fitting anything. The lens fitted on 54 documents had to read fresh text measurably better than the one fitted on 22. Three conditions all had to hold: an average gain above zero, a 95% confidence interval entirely above zero, and an average above 0.078, which is the spread we saw between document samples.

The paper's public data includes six sets of prompts, each with a hidden step that's known in advance. For example, "the color of the planet fourth from the Sun is" needs "Mars" before it can answer "red". The question is whether our lens surfaces the hidden step more often than the plain logit lens does. The pass line, and the controls that had to fail (a shuffled lens, and a decoy from another prompt in the same task set), were published before the run. It passed on 2026-11-21.

  1. The measure must be one the source paper uses for the claim we are testing.
  1. Before we spend anything on a forecast, we check it against data it wasn't fitted to.

What we got wrong, and what we learned

What we got wrong

  • We chose the wrong measure. Before Stage 2 we signed off on a bar for how closely the lens matches the model's own next-word prediction (KL divergence). The paper that introduced the lens says in its appendix that the lens scores worse than the plain logit lens on that measure through most of the network, and calls this "a feature rather than a defect". That sentence was in the paper before we started. We tested the lens on something it isn't built to do. What we learned: read a method's own stated limits before choosing the measure that judges it. (CLAIMS.md rows 7.2)
  • We spent GPU time on a forecast we hadn't tested. The case for Stage 2 rested on a curve fitted to lenses built from up to 22 documents, then stretched to 54, about two and a half times past anything we'd measured. It predicted a gain of 0.25 to 1.30. The run measured -0.041. Even the curve's best case left our lens behind the plain logit lens. We did write that down at the time, but not in the first line. Stage 2 used seven nights of GPU time. What we learned: don't spend on a curve stretched far past what was measured; test the stretch first, and put the risk in the first line. (CLAIMS.md rows 6.10, 7.2)
  • We found a bug in our own checker. Its answer-key copy of the model had no causal mask, so it could read ahead. The two tests that ran on it were withdrawn as proof. What we learned: the checker is tested too; we withdraw what it touched rather than keep it. (CLAIMS.md rows 4.1, 4.2)
  • Our first map, and a shortcut, failed their pass marks. The first map, from 6 documents, failed the first test we'd registered in advance: in layers 20 to 44 the lens found well under 1 of the next 10 tokens. A shortcut for building the map with random probes matched the exact map 70%, 60% and 37% of the time at three layers, against a pass mark of 90%. What we learned: a pass mark written in advance is what told us; we kept it rather than move it. (CLAIMS.md rows 2.2, 3.1)
  • More data did not help. The 54-document map didn't read fresh text any better than the 22-document map, and it failed all three conditions of the bar we'd written beforehand (average gain -0.041). What we learned: more data on this design did not help, so we are not scaling it further. (CLAIMS.md rows 7.2)

What we suspect

  • Whether the hidden step drives the answer. This shows the lens can read the hidden step. It doesn't show that the step drives the answer. That test is written down but has not run. (CLAIMS.md rows 8.1)
  • Why the first map failed. "The failure is about what we fed it, not how we built it" was a hypothesis, not a finding. (CLAIMS.md rows 2.9)
